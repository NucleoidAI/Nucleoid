
## Abstract

- A membership test gains Kotlin's negated spelling `A !in B`, an exact
  alias of the accepted `A not in B` of [NUC 97](nuc-0097.md). The parser
  reads `!` directly followed by the keyword `in`, with nothing between
  them, wherever it reads `not in`, and builds the same `not in` node, so
  evaluation, liveness, rendering and errors are the accepted ones. After
  `xs = [1, 2, 3]`, `[2 !in xs, 4 !in xs]` gives `[false, true]`, as
  `[2 not in xs, 4 not in xs]` does.
- Directly means on one line, with the `in` in the column right after the
  `!`, as Kotlin writes its single `!in` token. `! in` with whitespace, a
  comment or a line break between the two keeps its parse error, and `!`
  before any other token keeps its prefix reading, so `x !inx` is still the
  two statements `x` and `!inx`.
- At the accepted revision, every program in which `!` directly precedes
  `in` where a membership operator can stand fails to parse, with errors
  such as `SyntaxError("Unexpected keyword 'in'")` and
  `SyntaxError("Expected ']' but found '!'")`, and only those programs
  change. Inside a template interpolation, which ends before `not in` as it
  ends before `!in`, the accepted reading stays: `` `a ${4 !in xs} b` ``
  still gives `"a 4 b"`. The public Rust `parse_expression` changes only
  where NUC 97 changed it for `not in`: a Python-style conditional whose
  condition holds the test.
- A program written with `!in` shows `not in` in its rules, `why`,
  `affects`, `--graph` and graph keys, as programs written with `and`, `or`
  and `not` show `&&`, `||` and `!`.
- No keyword, lexer token, `BinaryOp`, syntax-tree variant, error type or
  message is added, and no public item changes. The authoritative contract
  is [Negated membership with !in](../nucleoid.spec.md#negated-membership-with-in).

## Motivation

- Kotlin writes negated membership as `!in`: `x !in xs`,
  `name !in reserved`, `"z" !in word`. Programmers, and the language models
  that write world models with Nucleoid, use it as readily as Python's
  `not in`, and Nucleoid already accepts both `in` and `not in` with the
  meaning Kotlin's `in` and `!in` have for lists, strings and class
  populations. Today each `!in` fails to parse, so a model's otherwise valid
  rule, such as `$Order.flagged = $Order.status !in approved`, stops the
  whole program before any statement runs.
- World models state exclusions as often as memberships: a user who is not
  blocked, an order whose status is not approved, a ticket whose status is
  not closed. These rules are standing, so they must follow every push to
  the list and every change of the status, which `not in` already does and
  the alias inherits.
- An alias keeps one membership operator in the runtime and one canonical
  spelling in rendered rules, as the accepted `and`, `or`, `not` and the
  Python-style conditional render as `&&`, `||`, `!` and `?:`, and advances
  the language-superset aspiration of NUC 1's Language compatibility
  category without changing any accepted form.

  `toList()`, `extend` and `count()`, which extend methods rather than

- **No counterparts.** Because comparison is skipped, the new cases have no
  accepted-syntax counterparts. They execute their assertions and explicit

  spelling gives on the accepted revision, and every pre-existing case is
  unchanged.
- **Optional evidence.** No hint was supplied. Advisory usefulness is
  unscored (`missing_rubric`), not zero. Strategy learning is
  `not_configured`, so this revision is unattributed, with no policy,
  selection receipt, trial or reward.
- **One node.** `A !in B` builds the very `Expr::Binary` with
  `BinaryOp::NotIn` that `A not in B` builds, so the two trees are equal and
  every path over the node applies unchanged: evaluation, dependency walks,
  `.value` freezing, class-level validation, rollback, reasoning, graph
  keys and rendering. Rejected:
  - a new `BinaryOp` variant, or a flag that records the spelling, which
    would let rules render `!in` but would change the public enum that
    exhaustive downstream matches use, and would give one test two graph
    keys and two `if` nodes;
  - a lexer token `!in`, which the longest-match lexer would also read at
    the start of `!inx`, so the accepted `x !inx`, two statements, would
    become `x !in x`, and which every reader of `!` would need to learn;
  - rewriting the source before lexing, which would also rewrite strings,
    comments and templates.
- **Two adjacent tokens, read by the parser.** `membership` reads the
  existing tokens `!` and `in` when the `in` begins on the line of the `!`,
  in the column right after it. A column counts the characters since the
  start of its line, so every character between two tokens on one line,
  whitespace or a block comment, takes a column, and the check means
  exactly that nothing lies between them. That is Kotlin's single `!in`
  token: `! in` is no operator there either. The parser already tells two
  tokens apart this way where they spell one form, an at-variable's `@` and
  its name, and the REPL's `@` marks do the same. Rejected: requiring only
  that `in` is the next token, which would read `! in`, `!` and a tab, and,
  inside brackets, where line breaks are no tokens, `!` at the end of one
  line and `in` at the start of the next.
- **Wherever `not in` is read.** `membership` is the one place that reads
  `not in`, so the alias shares by construction every context, the level of
  `<`, its chain limit of 128 operators, the step back before a colon
  outside a clause and the second token that `relational` consumes for
  `not in`. Operator sections read neither `in` nor `not in`, and `!` does
  not begin one, so `xs.filter(!in ys)` keeps its error.
- **The two ignored tops stay.** At the top level of a template
  interpolation and of `parse_expression`, membership tests are not formed,
  so `!` stays the first ignored token, as `not` does. A template such as
  `` `${x !in xs}` `` keeps reading `x`, the class of break that rejected
  [NUC 91](nuc-0091.md), while `` `${(x !in xs)}` `` tests membership.
- **Rendering as `not in`.** The tree records no spelling, so the test
  renders as `not in` wherever an expression is rendered: graph keys, the
  `rule` that `why` and `affects` report, `--graph` and the errors that
  quote an expression. That is how the accepted `and`, `or`, `not` and the
  Python-style conditional render: as `&&`, `||`, `!` and `?:`.
- **Only former parse errors.** At the accepted revision the parser reads
  `!` only as the prefix negation, whose operand cannot begin with the
  keyword `in`, and no other construct consumes a `!`. So every accepted
  program either has no `!` directly before an `in` or holds it among the
  ignored trailing tokens of an interpolation's top level, where the alias
  is not read either. No tentative parse turns such a pair into an accepted
  reading: a Python-style condition, a `suppose` premise and a loop header
  give up at the `!` and consume nothing, after which the statement parser
  fails at the same `!`; the consequent after `?` requires its `:`; an
  interpolation's top level attempts none of them; and the later parse
  passes read `!` and `in` with the same rule. The differential of
  [Preparation checks](#preparation-checks) confirms this on every
  accepted input it ran.
- **`parse_expression`.** Its top level keeps a tentative Python-style
  conditional, whose condition is a clause and so takes membership tests.
  [NUC 97](nuc-0097.md) recorded that
  `parse_expression("a if x in xs else b")` returned `a` before it and the
  conditional after it, as [NUC 62](nuc-0062.md) and
  [NUC 75](nuc-0075.md) recorded changes of the same kind before it. The
  alias is exact, so it brings the same change to the inputs that write
  `!in` there, and to no other input. No Nucleoid program, command-line run
  or REPL session can observe it: no production code calls
  `parse_expression`, which `src/lang/ast/parser.rs` defines and only
  tests call. Rejected: refusing `!in` inside a conditional at the top
  level of `parse_expression`, which would make the alias differ from
  `not in` there and need a new parser state outside `precedence.rs`.
- **Where the cases stand.** NUC 97's
  `no_earlier_document_case_holds_a_membership_test` forbids a membership
  test in any case before NUC 97's own, and its placement test pins
  NUC 98's cases directly after NUC 97's, so the new cases cannot stand
  before [NUC 103](nuc-0103.md)'s, as NUC 129's to NUC 131's do, or between
  NUC 97's and NUC 98's. They are appended at the end of each document,
  after [NUC 132](nuc-0132.md)'s at the accepted revision, as NUC 127's and
  NUC 132's were, which moves fewer count pins than any other place. The
  placement test requires only that they stand after NUC 97's and
  NUC 98's cases, together, as the last cases of each document. It names no
  other proposal's case, so it still holds when another proposal's cases
  come to stand before them, as [NUC 134](nuc-0134.md)'s, now on `main`,
  will on integration.
- **`Requires`.** NUC 97 specifies `not in`, its node and its contract.
- **Not added.** `!in` as an operator section; Kotlin's negated type test
  `!is`; `is !in`; ranges such as `x !in 1..5`, which Nucleoid has no
  literal for; key membership in objects; a lexer token or keyword; and any
  change to `in`, `not in`, `!`, `!=` or `!==`.

## Specification

- The normative contract is
  [Negated membership with !in](../nucleoid.spec.md#negated-membership-with-in).

### Syntax

- `ITEM !in COLLECTION`, where `!in` is the token `!` directly followed by
  the keyword `in`: the `in` begins on the line of the `!`, in the column
  right after it. It builds the tree of `ITEM not in COLLECTION`.
- It stands where `not in` stands, on the left-associative level of `<`,
  `<=`, `>`, `>=`, `in` and `not in`, and shares their chain limit of 128
  operators. It binds more tightly than `==`, `!=`, `===`, `!==`, `is`,
  `is not`, `and`, `&&`, `or`, `||`, `??`, `|>`, both conditional forms and
  assignment, and more loosely than `+`, `-`, `*`, `/`, `%`, `**` and every
  prefix operator, including `not`, `!`, `-`, `typeof`, noun queries and
  prefix stages:

| Source | Reads as |
| --- | --- |
| `x + 1 !in xs` | `(x + 1) !in xs` |
| `x !in xs == true` | `(x !in xs) == true` |
| `a < b !in c` | `(a < b) !in c` |
| `a !in b !in c` | `(a !in b) !in c` |
| `a in b !in c` | `(a in b) !in c` |
| `x !in xs and y !in ys` | `(x !in xs) and (y !in ys)` |
| `not x !in xs` | `(not x) !in xs` |
| `!x !in xs` | `(!x) !in xs` |
| `x !in why c` | `x !in (why c)` |
| `x !in sum of xs` | `x !in (sum of xs)` |
| `(a is b !in c)` | `(a is (b !in c))` |

- It applies wherever `not in` applies: declarations and assignment
  values, `if`, `elif`, colon-free and `while` conditions, `repeat ...
  until` conditions, class-level rules and conditionals, function and
  lambda bodies, call arguments, list items and object values, subscripts
  and slice bounds, `assert` in both spellings, `suppose` premises,
  comprehension sources and filters, quantifier collections and
  conditions, and the parenthesized, bracketed and called parts of a
  template interpolation.
- It is not formed at the top level of a template interpolation or of the
  public Rust `parse_expression` input, whose trailing tokens are ignored:
  `!` stays their first ignored token, as `not` does. Outside a clause it
  is not formed when `:` directly follows the right operand, so `!` and
  `in` keep their accepted readings and errors, as for `not in`.
- `!` followed by whitespace, a tab, a form feed, a carriage return, a
  comment or a line break before `in` is no operator: those programs keep
  their accepted errors. `!` before any other token keeps its prefix
  reading, so `!inx` negates the name `inx`, and `!(x in xs)` and
  `!x in xs`, which is `(!x) in xs`, keep theirs. `!=` and `!==` are tokens
  of their own and keep their meaning.

### Semantics

- `ITEM !in COLLECTION` is `ITEM not in COLLECTION` in everything:
  - `ITEM` is evaluated first, then `COLLECTION`;
  - a list, including a reasoning selection, does not hold `ITEM` when no
    item matches it under the deep equality of `includes`, a declared class
    when none of its instances does, and a string when the text of `ITEM`
    does not occur in it, so `"z" !in "hello"` is `true` and
    `"ell" !in "hello"` is `false`;
  - a null or undefined collection yields undefined, which a declaration
    stores as `null`, so a rule over a list that is not yet set waits for
    it;
  - any other collection raises `TypeError("<collection> is not a collection")`;
  - a declaration, `if` rule or class-level rule depends on what both
    operands read, so `missing = 4 !in xs` follows `xs` and
    `$Order.pending = $Order.status !in approved` follows each order's
    status and the list, and a failed standing re-evaluation rolls back the
    change that triggered it.
- These programs are specification entries. The first,
  `Nucleoid tests negated membership with Kotlin's !in`, returns `false`:

```
xs = [1, 2, 3]
assert([2 !in xs, 4 !in xs], [false, true])
assert([2 !in xs, 4 !in xs], [2 not in xs, 4 not in xs])
word = "hello"
assert(["z" !in word, "ell" !in word], [true, false])
class Seat
front = Seat()
assert([front !in Seat, 3 !in Seat], [false, true])
assert([1 + 3 !in xs, 4 !in xs == true, 2 !in xs or 4 !in xs], [true, true, true])
assert([!(4 in xs), 4 not in xs, 4 !in xs], [true, true, true])
missing = 4 !in xs
xs.push(4)
assert(missing, false)
missing
```

- `Nucleoid keeps !in rules live over class populations` returns
  `["lost","held"]`: each order's `pending` follows its status and the
  `approved` list, the filtered population follows them all, and writing a
  number into `approved` raises the `TypeError` and rolls back:

```
class Order(status):
    this.status = status
approved = ["paid", "shipped"]
$Order.pending = $Order.status !in approved
first = Order("new")
second = Order("paid")
assert([first.pending, second.pending], [true, false])
approved.push("new")
assert([first.pending, second.pending], [false, false])
first.status = "lost"
assert(first.pending, true)
open = Order.filter(order -> order.pending).map(order -> order.status)
assert(open, ["lost"])
second.status = "held"
assert(open, ["lost", "held"])
failure = ""
try:
    approved = 5
catch error:
    failure = error
assert(failure, TypeError("approved is not a collection"))
assert([approved, open], [["paid", "shipped", "new"], ["lost", "held"]])
open
```

- `Nucleoid rolls back failed updates through !in` returns `false`: a
  standing declaration over a list that a write turns into an object raises
  the `TypeError`, the write rolls back, and a null collection waits:

```
items = ["a"]
absent = "b" !in items
assert(absent, true)
failure = ""
try:
    items = {a: 1}
catch error:
    failure = error
assert(failure, TypeError("items is not a collection"))
assert([items, absent], [["a"], true])
items.push("b")
assert(absent, false)
tags = null
untagged = "red" !in tags
assert(untagged, null)
tags = ["blue"]
assert(untagged, true)
absent
```

### Rendering

- The tree is that of `not in`, so the test renders as `not in` wherever an
  expression is rendered, grouped as membership tests are:

| Source | Rendering |
| --- | --- |
| `ok = x !in xs` | `ok=(x not in xs)` |
| `ok = !(x !in xs)` | `ok=!(x not in xs)` |
| `ok = (a !in b) !in c` | `ok=((a not in b) not in c)` |
| `ok = a < b !in c` | `ok=(a<b not in c)` |
| `ok = (a !in b) ** 2` | `ok=(a not in b)**2` |
| `if "b" !in tags:` | the `if` node `if(("b" not in tags))` |

- After `xs = [1, 2, 3]` and `r = 4 !in xs`, `(why r).map(s -> s.rule)` is
  `["r = (4 not in xs)", "xs = [1,2,3]"]`, `(affects xs).map(s -> s.node)`
  is `["r"]`, and `--graph` lists `r [variable]` reading `xs`. An `if` with
  the condition `2 !in xs` and one with `2 not in xs` are the same graph
  node. An error that quotes an expression quotes `not in`:
  `1 in (2 !in xs)` raises `TypeError("(2 not in xs) is not a collection")`.

### Cost

- The change is parse-time only. `relational` consults `membership` at
  most once for each operand it reads; where neither `in` nor `not in`
  matches, `membership` now also tests whether the token is `!` and the next
  one `in`, and only for that pair it compares their lines and columns.
  Evaluation, propagation and rendering run the accepted `not in` code,
  since the tree is the same.
- **Spot check in phase 1**, not a benchmark, on the machine otherwise
  idle. Times are in milliseconds.
  - In process: `parse_program` of a scratch program linked with each
    library in a release build, the median of 11 parses per call and the
    median of 7 interleaved calls, with the range of those calls.
  - Through the command line with `--json`: `nucleoid-phase1.exe` and the
    accepted revision's release build, the median of 7 interleaved runs,
    including process start, each capped at 1.5 GB and 120 seconds, with
    the range of those runs.
  - "Lines" holds 10,000 declarations `rN = N !in xs` on as many lines;
    "One line" holds 10,000 tests in one list on a single line of 129 KB,
    159 KB with `not in`; "Body" puts the 10,000 declarations in the body
    of a function that is never called; "Standing" re-evaluates one
    declaration under 1,000 pushes from a `while` loop.

| Program | `!in`, candidate | `not in`, candidate | `not in`, accepted |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Lines, parse in process | 15.6 (15.2 to 16.0) | 16.0 (15.8 to 16.4) | 16.0 (15.6 to 16.7) |
| One line, parse in process | 2,567 (1,826 to 3,175) | 3,321 (2,357 to 4,037) | 3,505 (2,266 to 3,742) |
| Body, CLI | 30.0 (29.5 to 43.9) | 30.0 (29.5 to 54.4) | 31.2 (29.6 to 45.3) |
| Lines, CLI | 123.7 (120.2 to 152.4) | 126.2 (123.4 to 142.0) | 126.8 (124.2 to 128.5) |
| Standing, CLI | 38.3 (35.8 to 46.2) | 36.7 (33.8 to 44.6) | 37.1 (33.7 to 84.8) |

- On ordinary lines, a program of 10,000 `!in` tests parses and runs in
  the time of its `not in` spelling on either build, within the spread of
  these runs; no difference is measurable, and none is expected from two
  token comparisons. The single long line is slower on both builds for a
  reason that predates this proposal: the lexer finds each token's column
  and indentation by scanning its line from the start, in
  `Lines::position` and `line_position`, so lexing one line costs more than
  linearly in its length, and the `!in` spelling, 30 KB shorter, lexes
  faster. The spread of that row is wide, and it shows nothing about the
  alias beyond that.
